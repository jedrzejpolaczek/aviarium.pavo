TPE - Tree-structured Parzen Estimator

How TPE actually works

A classic sequential Bayesian optimizer - one of P3Net's six opponents in this paper's ablation grid. From the one idea it's built around, through the three procedures needed to implement it, to a demo that genuinely computes in your browser and shows the moment the model starts to remember.

Laid out like a C4 diagram: context, then the big moving parts, then each one broken down in detail, then code, and finally a practical checklist.

1Context - the problem TPE answers

In one sentence: random search never remembers anything. TPE remembers everything it has measured so far, and uses that to guess better where to try next.

That's the entire difference. Random search samples points independently - the tenth point knows exactly as much about the previous nine as the first point did, which is nothing. TPE (Bergstra et al., NeurIPS 2011) splits the observations so far into “good” and “bad” after every evaluation, fits a separate density model to each group, and picks the next point where the good-observation model is dense and the bad-observation model is sparse. It's still a sequential optimizer, sample by sample, but every next sample is now conditioned on history, not random.

In this paper's ablation grid, TPE stands on the side of classic, sequential model-based optimizers - alongside random search, MO-BOHB, SH-EMOA, NSGA-Net, and NSGANetV2, all benchmarked against P3Net on JAHS-Bench-201 and NAS-HPO-Bench-II (full grid description in implementation/lib/README.md). Random search is the floor: no model at all. TPE is the first step up from that floor - there's a model, but it treats the configuration space as a bag of independent (or at best shallowly conditional) dimensions. That boundary - model versus no model - is this page's topic, before section 6 narrows it down to the specific blind spot TPE has against P3Net's linkage-aware approach.

2Big picture - one loop, three steps per iteration

In one sentence: observe, split, fit two densities, sample a dozen or so candidates, pick the one with the best ratio, evaluate for real, go back to the start.

PartJob
Quantile splitSorts the observations so far by objective value and sets a threshold γ separating the best from the rest.
Density estimators l(x), g(x)Two probability models - one over “good” points, one over “bad” ones - telling you where in the space each group tends to cluster.
Acquisition stepSamples candidates from l(x) and picks the one with the highest l(x)/g(x) ratio - the only moment an evaluation is actually spent.

3  Components - breaking down each step

The same three parts as above, now at the level of detail needed to implement them.

3.1The quantile split - where “good” ends

In one sentence: sort the observations by objective value and cut off the best γ of them - the rest go to “bad.”

Under minimization, “better” means a smaller objective value. With n observations, the threshold is k = max(1, round(γ·n)) - the first k after sorting go into the good set, the rest into bad. A typical γ is 0.10–0.25; the demo below uses γ=0.15. The smaller γ is, the sharper and more selective the “good” model - but also the fewer points it has to work with, especially in early iterations.

3.2The density estimators l(x) and g(x)

In one sentence: place a Gaussian kernel at every point in a group and sum them - the high points of that sum are where the group tends to cluster.

Classic TPE builds these densities hierarchically, dimension by dimension conditioned by the configuration space's dependency tree (hence “tree-structured” in the name) - if hyperparameter B only exists when hyperparameter A has a certain value, the tree reflects that, and a mixture of truncated Gaussians is fit separately at each such conditional node.

An honest simplification in this demo

The demo below and the pseudocode in section 5 use an independent kernel estimator per dimension, with a fixed bandwidth - no conditional tree structure and no adaptive bandwidth rule (e.g. Scott's/Silverman's). This is a real, honest version of the algorithm's core - split, two densities, ratio - but not the full implementation from Bergstra's paper. Exactly this difference - shallow, independent dimensions versus real dependency structure - comes back in section 6 as the point of contrast with P3Net.

3.3The acquisition step - sampling and ranking by ratio l/g

In one sentence: draw a handful of candidates from l(x), score each by l(x)/g(x), and take the winner to this iteration's one real evaluation.

Maximizing l(x)/g(x) is equivalent to maximizing expected improvement against the threshold γ in this model - there's no need to compute EI separately, just compare the two densities at the same points. Candidates are drawn from l(x) (not the whole space), so they're immediately concentrated where good observations already are; g(x) in the denominator penalizes those among them that also sit close to bad observations.

4.1The problem: a small, deceptive 1D objective function

The demo below minimizes a one-variable function x ∈ [0, 10] with two dips: a narrow, deep one around x≈3.2 (the global minimum) and a wide but shallower one around x≈7.6 (a local minimum, an order of magnitude worse). The rest of the space is fairly flat and close to the worst value. This is deliberately deceptive because the wide shallow dip is easy to hit at random, while the narrow deep one isn't; a good density model should eventually concentrate sampling around the latter.

The real function is drawn as a thin dashed line purely for you as the reader - the algorithm itself never sees it, just as no black-box optimizer sees the shape of what it's optimizing.

5  Pseudocode - what you'd actually have to write

Three procedures. Nothing else is needed to have a working (simplified) TPE.

procedure TPE-LOOP(f, γ, n_candidates, R, budget)
    history ← ∅
    for t in 1..R do          // bootstrap: the model has nothing to learn from yet
        x ← random point in domain
        y ← f(x)
        history.add((x, y))

    while len(history) < budget do
        good, bad ← SPLIT-BY-QUANTILE(history, γ)   // §3.1
        l ← FIT-DENSITY(good)                          // §3.2
        g ← FIT-DENSITY(bad)                            // §3.2

        best ← null; bestScore ← -∞
        for c in 1..n_candidates do      // §3.3
            x' ← SAMPLE-FROM(l)
            score ← l(x') / (g(x') + ε)
            if score > bestScore then
                best ← x'; bestScore ← score

        y ← f(best)                          // the only real evaluation this iteration
        history.add((best, y))

    return argmin_{(x,y) in history} y
procedure SPLIT-BY-QUANTILE(history, γ)
    sorted ← sort history by y ascending    // minimization: smaller y = better
    k ← max(1, round(γ · len(sorted)))
    good ← sorted[0 : k]
    bad  ← sorted[k : ]
    return good, bad
procedure FIT-DENSITY(points)
    // Simplification for this page: an independent kernel estimator
    // per dimension, fixed bandwidth bw. Real TPE builds hierarchical (tree-
    // -structured) distributions over the CONDITIONAL structure of the config space -
    // see the callout in §3.2.
    xs ← {x : (x, y) in points}
    return x ↦ (1 / (len(xs)·bw)) · Σ_{xi in xs} K((x − xi) / bw)   // K = Gaussian kernel

That's the entire core of the algorithm. A real TPE implementation (e.g. in Optuna or Hyperopt) adds to this: a conditional space structure (a tree that FIT-DENSITY navigates separately at each conditional node), adaptive per-dimension bandwidth selection, and support for mixed spaces - continuous, integer, and categorical at once. The core - split, two densities, ratio - stays the same.

6  What you design yourself - applying TPE to a real problem

The same split you already know from other algorithms in this series: the mechanism is fixed by the algorithm - you don't touch how the quantile split or acquisition work. What changes every time is how your problem plugs into that mechanism.

Decision pointFixed by TPEYou decide, per problem
Good/bad split Always by a quantile threshold over the sorted history - the top γ goes to l(x), the rest to g(x). The value of γ - smaller gives a sharper, more selective model at the cost of fewer points to fit, especially early on.
The l(x), g(x) density family Always two separate densities over the same kind of object - one for good, one for bad observations. The concrete form: the kernel and its bandwidth (fixed as in this demo, or adaptive via Scott's/Silverman's rule), and - for real mixed spaces - how to represent categorical and integer dimensions.
Acquisition - ratio l/g The next candidate always maximizes l(x)/g(x) among those sampled - the equivalent of expected improvement against the threshold γ. The number of candidates sampled per iteration - too few and the ranking is random, too many and you waste time on arithmetic that won't change the choice anyway.
Search-space encoding Not addressed - TPE assumes a ready-made vector of variables with defined types and ranges. How your problem becomes that vector. For P3Net: one gene per cell-graph edge plus one per (discretized) hyperparameter; for TPE that exact same vector additionally has to be described as an independent or shallowly conditional variable space.
Number of starting points R Always a purely random bootstrap first - the model has nothing to draw on while the history is empty. How many bootstrap points are enough before l(x)/g(x) becomes more reliable than random guessing - too few and early models are noise, too many and you waste budget that informed acquisition could already have used.
The blind spot this page reveals

TPE - even in its full, hierarchical version from the paper - builds a model over raw configuration values, dimension by dimension. The conditional structure (“B only exists when A has this value”) is known up front and encoded in the space's tree, but which genes tend to move together, correlated, in good solutions - what P3 discovers dynamically through the linkage tree (section 3.3 of the P3 page) - isn't represented here at all. For TPE, every dimension is a separate question to the model; for P3Net, a whole gene cluster that historically worked best together is copied as one unit, and the decision of which genes belong to the same cluster is learned from data, not encoded in the space's structure up front.

In other words: TPE answers the question “where in this space is dense-good, and where is dense-bad?” - and it does this well, sequentially, with memory. It doesn't answer the question “which dimensions of this space even make sense considered separately?” That question is only asked by P3Net's relative, linkage-aware surrogate.

✓Checklist for applying TPE to a new problem

  • Define the variable vector: type and range of each dimension (continuous, integer, categorical).
  • Choose γ - smaller gives a sharper “good” model, and fewer points early on.
  • Choose the kernel family and bandwidth selection rule for l(x) and g(x).
  • Set the number of bootstrap points R and the number of candidates sampled per iteration.
  • Check whether your space's dimensions are actually reasonably independent - if not, TPE will still work, but it will lose out on what P3Net exploits directly.
Source: J. Bergstra, R. Bardenet, Y. Bengio, B. Kégl, “Algorithms for Hyper-Parameter Optimization,” NeurIPS 2011 - section 4 for the mechanism described above.
