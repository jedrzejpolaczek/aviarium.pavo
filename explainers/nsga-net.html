NSGA-Net - evolutionary NAS with full training

How NSGA-Net actually works

Classic NSGA-II - non-dominated sorting plus crowding distance - applied directly to neural network architecture genotypes. From the one loop it's built around, through the four procedures needed to implement it, to a handful of decisions you still have to make yourself before running it on your own problem.

Laid out like a C4 diagram: context, then the big moving parts, then each one broken down in detail, then code, and finally a practical checklist.

1Context - where NSGA-Net stands in the NAS landscape

In one sentence: NSGA-Net takes NSGA-II - the exact same multi-objective algorithm that's served engineering optimization for twenty years - and runs it directly on architecture genotypes, with no shortcut in evaluation at all.

Every candidate mutation or crossover produces gets fully trained before the algorithm knows anything about its objectives (accuracy, FLOPs, latency - whatever you choose). No cheaper intermediate evaluation, no surrogate, no weight combining objectives into a single number. It's pure, textbook multi-objective evolution, applied to a space where one evaluation can mean hours of GPU training.

Place in this paper's ablation grid

This page is one of nine arms of the ablation grid comparing P3Net against six baseline methods (random search, TPE, MO-BOHB, SH-EMOA, NSGA-Net, NSGANetV2) on JAHS-Bench-201 and NAS-HPO-Bench-II. NSGA-Net is P3Net's closest relative in this grid: both are multi-objective evolutionary methods, both operate directly on the architecture genotype as a vector of discrete decisions. The difference is in what NSGA-Net doesn't have: no pyramid that grows on its own, and no surrogate to replace real training. You fix the population size up front, once, and pay a full evaluation for every candidate that setting produces.

2Big picture - one loop, four moving parts

In one sentence: a fixed-size population of N produces N offspring, all N are fully trained, parents and offspring merge into a pool of 2N, from which non-dominated sorting and crowding distance select exactly N back.

PartJob
Population (size N)A fixed number of fully evaluated architectures, set at the start and never changed during the run.
Tournament selection + variationTwo random parents, compared by rank and crowding distance, then uniform crossover and mutation - with no knowledge of the genotype's structure at all.
Full trainingEvery newly created offspring is genuinely trained from scratch before its objectives are known. Zero shortcuts.
Non-dominated sorting + crowding distanceSplits the combined parents+offspring pool into Pareto fronts, then picks the population's missing remainder by neighbor density on the boundary front.

Notice what's missing here: no local search at the level of a single genotype, no structure learned from the population, no mechanism that would let N grow or shrink during the run. Everything NSGA-Net “knows” about the problem passes through two narrow bottlenecks: the variation operator (3.2) and the Pareto comparison (3.4).

3  Components - breaking down each part

The same four parts as above, now at the level of detail needed to implement them.

3.1The genotype - architecture as a gene-by-gene comparable vector

In one sentence: the architecture becomes a vector of discrete design decisions - exactly as in P3/P3Net, just without any tree linking those decisions together.

A typical NAS genotype is a list of operations on cell-graph edges (e.g. conv3×3, skip, none) plus, optionally, a few discretized training hyperparameters. NSGA-Net imposes nothing beyond the genotype being encodable as a locus-by-locus comparable vector, decodable into a network that can be trained. This is exactly the same genotype shape P3Net uses - the difference only starts in what the algorithm does with that vector next.

One thing here differs from P3/P3Net, with consequences in section 3.2: NSGA-Net never computes which loci tend to move together. Every gene is just as independent from every other, as far as the variation operator is concerned - regardless of whether, in reality, two edges only make sense together.

3.2Variation - uniform crossover and mutation, without structure

In one sentence: for every gene separately - a coin flip decides which parent it comes from; a second flip decides whether to mutate it to something random at all.

Two parents come from a tournament: two random candidates from the population, the lower front rank wins (closer to F₁ = better), and a rank tie is broken by higher crowding distance (see 3.4) - a more precise statement of the same thing is in section 5, in the TOURNAMENT procedure. With two parents, uniform crossover goes through every locus independently and draws whether the child gets a value from parent A or parent B. Mutation then, with a separate per-locus probability, replaces a gene's value with a completely new, random one.

3.3Evaluation - full training, always, no exceptions

In one sentence: before NSGA-Net knows a candidate's objectives, that candidate has to be genuinely trained - there's no cheaper version of this step.

This is where NSGA-Net differs most sharply from the rest of this paper's ablation grid. MO-BOHB and SH-EMOA cut cost with fidelity (shorter training for weaker candidates). NSGANetV2 replaces training with a predictor. P3Net replaces it with a relative surrogate inside crossover. NSGA-Net does none of this - every single offspring from section 3.2, promising or not, gets a full training cycle before anything is known about it.

This isn't an oversight - it's exactly what makes NSGA-Net a useful baseline: it shows what multi-objective architecture evolution costs if you don't cheat on evaluation in any way. Section 4 below makes this cost visible live, as a growing counter.

3.4NSGA-II selection - non-dominated sorting and crowding distance

In one sentence: split the combined pool into non-domination layers, then - only where trimming is needed - keep candidates from the front's most sparsely occupied areas.

Candidate a dominates candidate b if a is no worse than b on every objective and strictly better on at least one. Front F₁ is the set of candidates dominated by nobody. F₂ is those dominated only by candidates in F₁. And so on - every subsequent front is “remove previous fronts, find the non-dominated among what's left.”

When the boundary front doesn't fit whole, who survives from it is decided by crowding distance: for every point on the front, measure how far its nearest neighbors on that front are, separately for each objective, and sum the normalized distances. Edge points (extreme quality or extreme cost) get infinity - they always pass. Points in densely occupied areas get a small value and drop out first.

5  Pseudocode - what you'd actually have to write

Five procedures. Nothing else is needed to have a working NSGA-Net.

procedure NSGA-NET
    P ← N random genotypes                    // §3.1 - you fix N, see §6
    EVALUATE-ALL(P)                              // §3.3, full training of EVERY one - real cost
    fronts ← FAST-NON-DOMINATED-SORT(P)
    for F in fronts do ASSIGN-CROWDING-DISTANCE(F, P)

    for g in 1..G do                             // G is also fixed by you, up front
        Q ← VARY(P)                              // §3.2, size N, not yet evaluated
        EVALUATE-ALL(Q)                          // full training of EVERY offspring again
        R ← P ∪ Q                                // 2N fully evaluated
        fronts ← FAST-NON-DOMINATED-SORT(R)

        P ← {}; i ← 0
        while |P| + |fronts[i]| ≤ N do
            ASSIGN-CROWDING-DISTANCE(fronts[i], R)
            P ← P ∪ fronts[i]; i ← i + 1
        ASSIGN-CROWDING-DISTANCE(fronts[i], R)   // boundary front - needs trimming
        sort fronts[i] by crowding distance, descending
        P ← P ∪ fronts[i][0 : N − |P|]            // fill up to exactly N
    return P                                    // Pareto front(s) of the final generation
procedure VARY(P)
    Q ← {}
    while |Q| < N do
        p1 ← TOURNAMENT(P); p2 ← TOURNAMENT(P)
        child ← []
        for locus in genotype do                  // §3.2 - uniform, independent per locus
            gene ← p1[locus] if random() < 0.5 else p2[locus]
            if random() < p_mut then
                gene ← random allowed value      // mutation, again independent per locus
            child.append(gene)
        Q.add(child)
    return Q
procedure TOURNAMENT(P)
    a, b ← two random, not necessarily distinct individuals from P
    if a.rank < b.rank then return a
    if b.rank < a.rank then return b
    return a if a.crowd > b.crowd else b       // a rank tie is broken by density
procedure FAST-NON-DOMINATED-SORT(R)
    for p in R do
        p.dominated_by ← 0; p.dominates ← {}
        for q in R, q ≠ p do
            if p ≻ q then p.dominates.add(q)         // ≻: no worse on both objectives, better on ≥1
            else if q ≻ p then p.dominated_by += 1
        if p.dominated_by == 0 then p.rank ← 0; F[0].add(p)
    i ← 0
    while F[i] ≠ ∅ do
        for p in F[i] do for q in p.dominates do
            q.dominated_by -= 1
            if q.dominated_by == 0 then q.rank ← i + 1; F[i+1].add(q)
        i ← i + 1
    return F                                    // §3.4
procedure ASSIGN-CROWDING-DISTANCE(F, R)
    for p in F do p.crowd ← 0
    for objective in {quality, cost} do
        sort F by objective
        F[0].crowd ← ∞; F[last].crowd ← ∞         // front edges always survive
        for k in 1..len(F)-2 do
            F[k].crowd += (F[k+1].objective − F[k-1].objective) / range(objective)

That's the whole algorithm. There's no procedure at all corresponding to a hill climber or the P3/P3Net linkage tree - the only mechanism that “learns” anything from the population is the choice of who enters the tournament, via rank and crowding distance.

6  What you design yourself - applying NSGA-Net to a real problem

The same split as in P3 and P3Net: the mechanism is fixed by the algorithm - you don't touch the non-domination rule or how crowding distance is computed. What changes every time you apply NSGA-Net to a new problem is how your problem plugs into that mechanism - and, in this case, two numbers you have to guess before you see even one result.

Decision pointFixed by NSGA-NetYou decide, per problem
Non-domination rule Candidate a dominates b when it's no worse on every objective and strictly better on at least one - comparing both objectives at once, never a weighted sum. Nothing - this is the part you get for free by choosing this algorithm over single-objective optimization with scalarization.
Breaking rank ties Crowding distance - the sum of normalized distances to the nearest neighbors on the front, per objective separately. Nothing directly, but you decide how many objectives you have and how they're normalized - if one objective has a much wider value range than another, crowding distance favors it unless you normalize carefully.
Population size N Not addressed - the algorithm assumes you already have N. You fix it once, up front, before the first evaluation happens. Too small N - the Pareto front too sparse to conclude anything. Too large - every generation costs proportionally more full training runs.
Number of generations G Not addressed. Also up front. Together with N it determines the total budget: exactly N + N·G full training runs, no more and no less, regardless of how quickly the front stopped improving.
Variation operators Not addressed - NSGA-II as such doesn't impose a specific crossover or mutation. The type of crossover (uniform? single-point?), the per-locus mutation probability - and, for architectures, whether uniform crossover even makes sense if some genes have to change together to improve anything.
Genotype encoding Not addressed - it only requires a vector comparable locus by locus. Exactly the same problem as in P3/P3Net: how your architecture becomes that vector, and what happens to genotypes that don't decode into a working network.
The two gaps that actually create P3Net

NSGA-Net exposes exactly the two blind spots the rest of this ablation grid attacks from different angles - and P3Net attacks both at once, with the same mechanism. First: you have to guess N and G up front, before you see even one result - exactly the same problem P3's parameter-less pyramid solves for the single-objective case, letting the “population” size emerge from however many levels actually got built. Second: NSGA-Net pays a full, real evaluation for every single candidate variation produces, with no relative shortcut at all - exactly the cost P3Net attacks with the surrogate δ̂F inside linkage-aware crossover, paying a real evaluation only once per whole sweep, instead of once per offspring.

In other words: NSGA-Net is P3Net's closest relative in this grid exactly because it differs from it in only these two things - and both are exactly what P3Net was designed to solve.

✓Checklist for applying NSGA-Net to a new problem

  • Define the genotype: what one gene is, how many there are, and whether some combinations are invalid.
  • Define every objective separately and how it's measured - never as a single weighted number.
  • Choose N and G - and immediately calculate how many full training runs N + N·G costs against your budget.
  • Choose the crossover and mutation operators; check whether uniform crossover makes sense for your encoding, or destroys dependencies between genes.
  • Normalize objectives before computing crowding distance, so none of them dominates the diversity selection just because it has a wider range.
  • If N + N·G full training runs doesn't fit your budget, that's a signal to consider a surrogate or a method that doesn't fix N and G up front - not an NSGA-Net parameter to tune, but a different point in the ablation grid.
