MO-BOHB - Multi-Objective Bayesian Optimization and Hyperband

How MO-BOHB actually works

Two mechanisms stitched into one algorithm: a Hyperband-style budget ladder that cuts cost through cheap, low-fidelity evaluations, and a multi-objective Bayesian Optimization model (TPE) that replaces random sampling with a smarter choice of what to even try. Either one alone is already an existing algorithm - MO-BOHB is a specific way of combining them.

Laid out like a C4 diagram: context, then the big moving parts, then each one broken down in detail, then code, and finally a practical checklist.

1Context - the problem MO-BOHB answers

In one sentence: fully evaluating one candidate is expensive, so instead of paying for it every time, MO-BOHB pays little for an early screening and a lot only for a handful of finalists - and which candidates even enter the screening is chosen by a model learned from results so far, not a coin flip.

In neural architecture search or hyperparameter tuning, “evaluating a candidate” most often means “training a network.” That can take minutes, hours, sometimes days. Random search and classic evolutionary algorithms pay this price for every candidate, regardless of how hopeless it turns out to be after just a few epochs. Hyperband (Li et al., 2018) notices that a short, cheap training run is usually enough to tell very weak candidates apart from the rest - so it trains many candidates briefly, keeps only the top fraction, and only then gives them more budget. BOHB (Falkner et al., 2018) adds the other half: instead of randomly choosing who even enters the screening, it trains a model on all evaluations so far and samples closer to regions that have already proven promising. MO-BOHB is a version of the same idea for multiple objectives at once (e.g. accuracy and inference cost) instead of one.

MO-BOHB's place in this project

This page describes one of the six arms of the ablation grid P3Net is compared against in the experiments for the GECCO 2027 paper - alongside NSGA-Net, NSGANetV2, SH-EMOA, TPE, and random search, on JAHS-Bench-201 and NAS-HPO-Bench-II. MO-BOHB represents the family of methods that cut cost through cheaper partial-fidelity evaluations and try to rescue sampling quality with a model - unlike P3Net, which cuts cost in a completely different way: a relative, linkage-aware surrogate, but always at the full, target evaluation semantics. Section 6 returns to this contrast.

Before we go into detail, here's what the difference looks like at the budget level: classic search pays the same, full cost for every candidate; MO-BOHB splits the budget unevenly, so most of the money goes where it's already known to be worthwhile.

2Big picture - one bracket, one loop

In one sentence: every “bracket” is one full ladder of rounds - from sampling to a winner - and what MO-BOHB adds to plain Hyperband is the way candidates entering the first round are chosen.

3  Components - breaking down each part

The same three parts as above, now at the level of detail needed to implement them.

3.1The fidelity ladder - how many rounds, how much budget, how many candidates

In one sentence: the budget grows geometrically by a factor η every round, and the number of candidates shrinks by exactly the same factor - the two numbers are coupled.

Before a bracket even starts, three numbers have to be set: the minimum budget b_min (the cheapest, least reliable evaluation - e.g. one training epoch), the maximum budget b_max (the full, target evaluation - e.g. a hundred epochs), and the trimming factor η (usually 2 or 3). The number of rounds follows from how many times b_min has to be multiplied by η to reach b_max. The starting number of candidates is chosen so that the population is divided by η every round and ends with one survivor after the last round.

This is exactly the ladder the demo in section 4 runs on (η=3). Notice the total cost: 9·1 + 3·3 + 1·9 = 27 budget units for the whole bracket, versus 9·9 = 81 if all nine got the full budget right away.

Real Hyperband (and BOHB/MO-BOHB) runs several such ladders in parallel with different starting points - one starts from a very cheap, wide population, another straight from a pricier, narrower one - to guard against a cheap evaluation being misleading for that particular problem. This page, for clarity, shows one representative ladder shape; the mechanics of successive rounds are identical no matter how many such ladders you run at once.

3.2The multi-objective BO/TPE sampler - where new candidates come from

In one sentence: split the observations so far into “good” and “the rest” by a quantile threshold γ, fit a density to each group, and sample where the “good” density is high relative to “the rest.”

TPE (Tree-structured Parzen Estimator) doesn't model the objective function directly - it models the distribution of configurations. All observations so far are sorted by score and split by a quantile threshold γ (e.g. the best 30%) into two groups: “good” l(x) and “the rest” g(x). A probability density over the configuration space is fit for each group. A new candidate is sampled to maximize the ratio l(x)/g(x) - that is, where it's dense among the good ones and sparse among the rest.

For multiple objectives at once (accuracy and cost, say), there's no single obvious way to define “good” - multiple objectives first have to be reduced to one rank. Two honest simplifications worth knowing: scalarization (one score weighted as a sum of objectives, possibly with randomized weights between iterations, to cover the whole Pareto front) or a separate model per objective plus a rule combining their rankings (e.g. non-domination). This page, for the demo's clarity, uses simple scalarization with fixed weights - a simplification openly admitted, not hidden as something more.

3.3The promotion rule - successive halving

In one sentence: after every round, sort by score and keep only the top fraction 1/η - the rest drops out with no right to a pricier evaluation.

This is the whole trimming mechanism: no negotiation, no absolute score threshold, just a relative ranking within the current round. A candidate that did great in round 0 purely because the cheap evaluation's noise happened to favor it may still get promoted - the ladder doesn't guard against a single measurement error, only against systematically wasting budget on candidates that are consistently weak across many fidelity levels. This assumption - that a cheap evaluation correlates with an expensive one well enough for the ranking to make sense - is one section 6 returns to as a limitation of this whole family of methods.

4.1The problem: a two-dimensional space with a hidden landscape

The demo below searches a two-dimensional configuration space (x, y) ∈ [0,1]² - imagine two normalized hyperparameters. The true quality landscape has two hills in different parts of the space (two notional objectives, scalarized with 0.5/0.5 weights into one score) - but the algorithm never sees this landscape directly. It only sees individual, noisy readings whose noise shrinks with the round's budget: the cheap evaluation in round 0 is an uncertain approximation, the full budget in the last round is close to the truth.

5  Pseudocode - what you'd actually have to write

Four procedures. Nothing else is needed to have a working MO-BOHB.

procedure MO-BOHB
    observations ← ∅
    loop forever                          // one loop pass = one bracket
        budgets ← [b_min, b_min·η, …, b_max]     // §3.1
        n0 ← number of candidates at entry, so that 1/η per round reaches 1
        configs ← SAMPLE-BATCH(n0, observations) // §3.2, random if observations = ∅

        for r in 0 … len(budgets)-1 do
            for c in configs do
                c.score ← EVALUATE(c, budget=budgets[r])
                observations.add((c, budgets[r], c.score))
            configs ← TOP-FRACTION(configs, 1/η)  // §3.3, sort descending by score, trim

        winner ← configs[0]                       // the only survivor after the last round
procedure SAMPLE-BATCH(n, observations)
    if observations = ∅ then
        return [ RANDOM-POINT() for _ in 1…n ]   // the first bracket is always random

    score ← SCALARIZE(objectives)                  // §3.2: YOU choose the weights or a per-objective model
    good, rest ← SPLIT-BY-QUANTILE(observations, γ, score)
    l ← FIT-DENSITY(good)
    g ← FIT-DENSITY(rest)

    batch ← []
    for _ in 1…n do
        x ← DRAW arg max l(x) / g(x)                // sample where “good” is dense, “the rest” sparse
        batch.add(x)
    return batch
procedure TOP-FRACTION(configs, frac)
    sort configs descending by score
    k ← ceil(len(configs) · frac)
    return configs[0 : k]                          // the rest drops out, no right to a pricier round
procedure EVALUATE(c, budget)
    // YOU define what budget even means: epochs, data-subset size,
    // image resolution… - the only requirement is monotonicity: more budget,
    // less noise, closer to the true, target result.
    return evaluate_candidate(c, fidelity=budget)

That's the whole algorithm. There's no crossover or mutation procedure at all, no population pyramid - the structure is a flat sequence of rounds within one bracket, and the only memory carried between brackets is the growing observations list, which SAMPLE-BATCH uses.

6  What you design yourself - applying MO-BOHB to a real problem

The same split as the other pages in this series: the mechanism is fixed by the algorithm - you don't touch how trimming works or how l(x)/g(x) is computed. What changes every time is how your problem plugs into that mechanism - and, for MO-BOHB, what exactly “cheaper” means.

Decision pointFixed by MO-BOHBYou decide, per problem
Promotion rule After every round: sort by (scalarized) score, keep the top 1/η, drop the rest with no appeal. Nothing - this is the part you get for free, exactly the same for any problem.
BO/TPE sampling mechanism A quantile split into “good”/“the rest,” density fitting for both groups, sampling that maximizes l(x)/g(x). Nothing in the mechanism itself - but see below, since what this mechanism receives as input is already your decision.
The fidelity dimension Not addressed - the algorithm assumes such a dimension exists and monotonically approaches the truth as budget grows. What “a cheap evaluation” even means for your problem: number of epochs, training-set fraction, input resolution - and how large b_min/b_max need to be so the low-budget ranking isn't pure noise.
The factor η and the number of rounds Not addressed - these are the algorithm's own hyperparameters. Higher η = more aggressive, cheaper screening, but a bigger risk of dropping a good candidate due to low-fidelity noise. Lower η = safer, but pricier.
Multi-objective scalarization Not addressed - TPE needs a single rank to even split observations into “good” and “the rest.” Fixed weights, randomized weights between samplings (to cover the whole Pareto front), or a separate density model per objective with a rule combining rankings - each option distorts what “good” even means differently.
Search-space encoding Not addressed - TPE works over any space, as long as you can fit densities and sample over it. How your architecture genotype (cell-graph edges, discrete hyperparameters) becomes a point for which l(x) and g(x) can even be computed.
The blind spot this page reveals for P3Net

MO-BOHB cuts cost two ways at once, and both share an assumption in the background: that a low-fidelity cheap evaluation correlates with the true, full-scale result strongly enough for the ranking to make sense, and that the configuration space can be sensibly modeled as points in some metric - with no knowledge at all of which dimensions of that space are structurally linked. The TPE model treats every hyperparameter separately (or flatly, as one vector) - it doesn't know, and can't know, that two genes of an architecture genotype tend to change together, because it never builds anything resembling a linkage tree.

P3Net cuts cost via a completely different route: it doesn't retreat to a cheaper, approximate fidelity - it stays at the full, target evaluation semantics the whole time - and instead exploits the genotype's structure, learning which genes are linked (the linkage tree), and cheaply estimating only the difference between a candidate and its parent along those specific, learned gene groups. In other words: MO-BOHB asks “how cheaply can I evaluate any candidate individually,” P3Net asks “which parts of the genotype are even worth evaluating together” - and that second question is never asked in MO-BOHB, no matter how many brackets you run.

✓Checklist for applying MO-BOHB to a new problem

  • Define the fidelity dimension: what “cheap” evaluation means for your problem, and whether a low budget correlates with the full one at all.
  • Choose b_min, b_max, and η - check how many rounds and starting candidates follow from them.
  • Decide on multi-objective scalarization or a per-objective model with a rule combining rankings.
  • Encode the search space as something density l(x) and g(x) can be fit against.
  • Check whether low fidelity is systematically misleading for your problem - if so, this is a real limitation of the whole method family, not a parameter to tune.
Source: L. Li et al., “Hyperband: A Novel Bandit-Based Approach to Hyperparameter Optimization,” JMLR 2018; S. Falkner, A. Klein, F. Hutter, “BOHB: Robust and Efficient Hyperparameter Optimization at Scale,” ICML 2018 - the multi-objective extension (MO-BOHB) combines the same Hyperband ladder with multi-objective TPE.
