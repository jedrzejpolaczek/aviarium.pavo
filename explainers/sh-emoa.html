SH-EMOA - Successive Halving Evolutionary Multi-Objective Algorithm

How SH-EMOA actually works

One of the seven arms in the P3Net paper's ablation table - ordinary multi-objective evolution (Pareto sorting + crowding distance), with one thing added: the evaluation budget is cut inside every generation, in waves of fidelity reduction, before anyone even reaches Pareto sorting.

Laid out like a C4 diagram: context, then the big moving parts, then each one broken down in detail, then code, and finally a practical checklist.

1Context - two problems at once, one algorithm

In one sentence: SH-EMOA is an NSGA-II-like multi-objective evolution in which every offspring evaluation is first tested cheaply, and only the survivors get a full, expensive assessment.

Take any standard EMOA (evolutionary multi-objective algorithm) - a population, offspring through mutation and crossover, selection into the next generation based on Pareto non-domination and crowding distance, exactly like NSGA-II. Now assume a single evaluation is expensive - for us: a full neural network training run. Fully evaluating every offspring in every generation quickly becomes unaffordable. SH-EMOA answers this exactly the way Hyperband answers the same problem in a single-objective context: instead of training every candidate fully, train cheaply, stop the weaker half, train the survivors a bit longer, repeat - until only a handful remain that deserve the full budget.

Place in the ablation table

In the nine-armed comparison grid from the P3Net paper (JAHS-Bench-201, NAS-HPO-Bench-II; see implementation/lib/README.md), SH-EMOA stands alongside NSGA-Net, NSGANetV2, MO-BOHB, TPE, and random search. It's the arm that combines evolution with budget cutting - but without learning any structure. Mutation and crossover stay naive: no information at all about which genes of the architecture genotype are functionally linked. This is exactly the gap P3Net's linkage tree targets - the algorithm below shows what a multi-objective search looks like that cuts evaluation cost just as cleverly as P3Net, but still recombines blindly.

In other words: SH-EMOA is the product of two separate, well-known ideas - Pareto selection (how to know what's “better” when there's more than one objective) and the fidelity ladder (how to know who's even worth evaluating fully). The rest of this page breaks down both, separately and together.

2Big picture - one loop, two mechanisms

In one sentence: every generation creates offspring naively, sieves them through the fidelity ladder, and only what survives to full evaluation competes for a spot in the next population via Pareto sorting and crowding distance.

PartJob
Naive variationCrossover and mutation with no knowledge of the genotype's structure at all - every gene treated independently of the rest.
Fidelity ladderSuccessive, increasingly expensive evaluation levels; after every level half the candidates drop out, before anyone gets the full training budget.
Pareto selectionThose who make it to full evaluation join a shared pool with the parents; non-domination sorting and crowding distance pick the next population.

3  Components - breaking down each part

The same three parts as above, now at the level of detail needed to implement them.

3.1Naive variation operators - crossover and mutation without structure

In one sentence: every gene is treated as if it has nothing to do with any other gene - because, for these operators, it truly doesn't.

Crossover is usually uniform: for every gene position, independently draw whether the value comes from parent A or parent B. Mutation is usually independent per gene: with a fixed probability p_m, perturb the value at that one locus, without looking at the rest of the genotype. Neither operator knows, or tries to find out, which genes tend to move together - there's no frequency table, no linkage tree, no clusters.

This works fine as long as the objective function is roughly separable - improving one gene on its own genuinely moves you toward a better solution. It fails exactly where several genes have to change together to see any improvement at all (exactly the same problem the trap function in the P3 guide illustrates) - uniform crossover with a 50% chance per gene almost never copies a whole such group at once, so the beneficial combination practically never arises.

This is exactly the gap P3Net attacks

In a neural network architecture genotype, genes describing, say, two edges of the same computational cell often have to change together to produce a sensible, better architecture - a single edge change alone can be neutral or harmful. Uniform crossover has no idea these two genes are linked; P3Net learns this explicitly through the linkage tree and recombines whole learned clusters at once. Section 6 returns to this with a concrete comparison.

3.2The fidelity ladder - the halving rule

In one sentence: evaluate cheaply, stop the worse half, repeat on a more expensive level, until only as many candidates remain as you can afford to evaluate fully.

The ladder is a sequence of fidelity levels of increasing cost - e.g. training on 1/9, then 1/3, then all of the training data; or 10, then 30, then 100 epochs. At every level, every still-alive candidate gets an evaluation at that level, candidates are ranked, and the top half (hence “successive halving”) moves on to the next, more expensive level. The rest are dropped - they get no further chance in this generation.

The key design decision: what the ranking at an intermediate level even is, given that there are two (or more) objectives, and full Pareto sorting only makes sense on fully evaluated points. The typical solution - also used in the section 4 demo - is a simple scalar proxy score (e.g. a sum of normalized objectives, or hypervolume contribution) just for the purposes of one elimination wave; proper, full Pareto sorting with crowding distance is reserved for once candidates already have real, full evaluations.

3.3Selection - non-domination sorting and crowding distance

In one sentence: build the new population front by front from the best, and when a front doesn't fit entirely, keep the individuals spread furthest apart in objective space from it.

Individual x dominates individual y if x is no worse than y on any objective and strictly better on at least one. Front 1 is the set of individuals dominated by nobody in the pool. Remove it, recompute domination on what's left - that's front 2. Repeat. The new population fills up front by front from the first, as long as the next whole front fits.

The problem arises when the boundary front doesn't fit entirely - some of its individuals have to be picked and the rest dropped, even though they're formally equal (none dominates any other). Crowding distance breaks this tie: for each objective separately, sort the front by that objective's value; the front's edges (smallest and largest value) get infinite distance (always kept), and every middle individual gets the sum of normalized distances to its neighbors on each objective. Higher distance = less crowded neighborhood = priority in selection. This keeps the front spread evenly along the whole trade-off curve, instead of clumping in one place.

4  See it in action

Not an animation of one fixed example - this is SH-EMOA actually running, computing in your browser, on a two-objective test function similar to ZDT1.

4.1The problem: what SH-EMOA actually has to prove itself on

The genotype is six real-valued genes in [0, 1]: x₀ and five “distance genes” x₁…x₅. The first objective is simply f₁ = x₀ - smaller is better. The second objective depends on how close to zero the distance genes are: g = 1 + 9 · average(x₁…x₅), f₂ = g · (1 − √(f₁/g)) - also smaller is better. The true Pareto front lies where all distance genes are close to zero (g ≈ 1); any distance gene that stays high drags the whole front up and to the right, i.e. toward worse solutions.

The fidelity ladder trick: low fidelity computes g from just one randomly chosen distance gene instead of all five - exactly as a cheap architecture evaluation might train on a small data subset instead of the whole thing. Medium fidelity uses three genes. Full fidelity uses all five and gives the true value. The fewer genes a given level sees, the noisier (but still correlated) its estimate is - this is exactly the cost/accuracy trade-off the whole ladder is meant to exploit.

4.2The solution: SH-EMOA actually run on this problem

The parent population has 8 individuals. Every generation creates 12 offspring by uniform crossover (a random pair of parents, each gene independently from one of them) and independent per-gene mutation. The fidelity ladder sieves them 12→6→3 (exactly as in the 3.2 diagram), and the 3 full-fidelity finalists join the 8 parents in a pool of 11 candidates. From there, Pareto sorting and crowding distance select the 8 individuals of the new population.

Step by step you see both mechanisms separately: the elimination wave (who drops out before anyone pays the full price) and Pareto selection (who stays in the population once everyone already has real scores). These are two different questions, solved by two different mechanisms of the same algorithm.

5  Pseudocode - what you'd actually have to write

Four procedures. The first two are plain NSGA-II; the third is the only thing SH-EMOA adds.

procedure SH-EMOA
    P ← INIT-POPULATION(μ)                    // μ random genotypes
    EVALUATE-FULL(P)                           // every individual evaluated at full fidelity
    (F1, F2, …) ← NON-DOMINATED-SORT(P)        // §3.3
    while evaluation budget not exhausted do
        O ← ∅
        for i in 1..λ do
            pa, pb ← pick 2 parents from P (tournament by (rank, crowding))
            child ← CROSSOVER(pa, pb)          // §3.1, uniform, no structure
            child ← MUTATE(child)              // §3.1, independent per gene
            O.add(child)
        O ← FIDELITY-LADDER(O)                 // §3.2 - this is where halving happens
        Q ← P ∪ O                              // parents + those who made it to full fidelity
        (F1, F2, …) ← NON-DOMINATED-SORT(Q)
        P ← ∅; i ← 1
        while |P| + |Fi| ≤ μ do
            ASSIGN-CROWDING-DISTANCE(Fi)
            P ← P ∪ Fi; i ← i + 1
        if |P| < μ then
            ASSIGN-CROWDING-DISTANCE(Fi)
            sort Fi descending by crowding distance
            P ← P ∪ first (μ − |P|) of Fi     // filling in at the front boundary
    return F1                                  // non-dominated front of the final population
procedure FIDELITY-LADDER(O)
    for rung r in [cheap, medium, …, full] do
        for x in O do
            score(x) ← EVALUATE-AT(x, r)        // a cheaper, biased evaluation; YOU define this cost
        if r ≠ full then
            O ← top half of O by score(r)      // halving - scalar ranking, not full Pareto
        else
            EVALUATE-FULL(O)                    // only now the real (f1, f2, …)
    return O                                   // those who made it to full fidelity
procedure NON-DOMINATED-SORT(Q)
    for x in Q do x.dominatedBy ← 0; x.dominates ← ∅
    for x, y in Q × Q, x ≠ y do
        if x ≺ y then y.dominatedBy += 1; x.dominates.add(y)
    F1 ← { x ∈ Q : x.dominatedBy = 0 }
    i ← 1
    while Fi ≠ ∅ do
        Fi+1 ← ∅
        for x in Fi do
            for y in x.dominates do
                y.dominatedBy −= 1
                if y.dominatedBy = 0 then Fi+1.add(y)
        i ← i + 1
    return (F1, F2, …)
procedure ASSIGN-CROWDING-DISTANCE(F)
    for x in F do x.crowd ← 0
    for each objective m do
        sort F ascending by objective m's value
        F[first].crowd ← F[last].crowd ← ∞      // front edges always preferred
        for i in 2..|F|−1 do
            F[i].crowd += (F[i+1].m − F[i−1].m) / (max_m − min_m)
    return F                                        // sorted descending by crowd at selection time

That's the whole algorithm. There's no procedure at all that learns the genotype's structure - CROSSOVER and MUTATE work identically regardless of whether genes are linked or not.

6  What you design yourself - applying SH-EMOA to a real problem

The selection mechanism (Pareto + crowding) and the budget-cutting mechanism (halving) are fixed by the algorithm. What changes every time you plug this into a new problem is the genotype, the variation operators, and the ladder itself.

Decision pointFixed by SH-EMOAYou decide, per problem
Non-domination rule x dominates y if x is no worse on any objective and strictly better on at least one; fronts are built iteratively from F1. Which objectives even enter the comparison, and whether each is minimized or maximized (e.g. accuracy to maximize, cost to minimize - this has to be unified before comparison).
Tie-break on the front Crowding distance: the sum of normalized distances to neighbors on each objective, front edges always kept. Nothing - this is the one point in the table usually left unchanged even between problems.
Ladder promotion rule The top half of the ranking at a given level moves on to the next, more expensive level; the rest drops out with no further chance this generation. What the proxy ranking at an intermediate level even is, given that full Pareto doesn't work there yet - scalar, hypervolume, something else.
Genotype A vector of values, compared solely through the evaluation result. How your problem becomes that vector - for a network architecture: genes for cell-graph edges plus genes for training hyperparameters.
Crossover and mutation Uniform crossover, independent per-gene mutation - a scheme, not concrete parameters. The mutation probability, the perturbation strength, possibly a different crossover operator - but no “structure-aware” version without going beyond the algorithm itself.
Ladder budgets and halving rate The number of levels, and that each one drops half, is the scheme - the concrete numbers aren't. How many levels, at what cost each (epochs, data subset, proxy-network size), and whether the reduction factor really has to be 2.
Population size μ and offspring λ Not fixed by the algorithm - unlike P3, SH-EMOA doesn't derive them automatically. Both have to be chosen up front, just as in a plain GA - SH-EMOA doesn't solve this problem, only the evaluation-cost one.
The blind spot this page reveals

SH-EMOA cuts evaluation cost exactly the same way MO-BOHB does - via the fidelity ladder - and does multi-objective selection correctly, with full Pareto sorting and crowding distance. But the variation operators (§3.1) have no idea at all which genes of the genotype are functionally linked. Uniform crossover recombines genes one at a time and blindly - if a good architecture requires changing three specific edges together, the probability that uniform crossover copies exactly those three at once from the same parent drops exponentially with the number of genes.

This is exactly the gap P3Net targets: the linkage tree learns which genes usually change value together, and linkage-guided crossover recombines whole learned clusters at once, instead of individual genes. SH-EMOA and P3Net can both cut evaluation cost with the same kind of trick (fidelity ladder vs. relative surrogate) - the difference that actually decides the quality of the architectures found lies in whether recombination even knows what to change together.

✓Checklist for applying SH-EMOA to a new problem

  • Define the genotype and search space, just as for any GA.
  • Define all objectives and unify the optimization direction (minimize or maximize) for each.
  • Design the fidelity ladder: how many levels, at what real cost each, and whether the cost difference between levels actually translates into savings.
  • Decide what the proxy ranking at intermediate levels even is, given that full Pareto sorting doesn't make sense there yet.
  • Choose μ, λ, and the halving rate - SH-EMOA won't guess these for you.
  • If you suspect strong gene linkage (and in architecture genotypes there usually is), that's a real design problem, not a parameter to tune - P3Net's answer: the linkage tree and linkage-guided crossover.
